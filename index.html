About

It started with banana bread and a spreadsheet.

My mom used to bake. It started with her carrying things over to the park, and people kept asking to buy them, and somewhere in there it turned into an actual little business. Every Sunday she was up at five, baking and working out orders with a pencil and whatever Post-it or scrap of paper was nearest. This person bought four banana breads, so they owe this much. Then the carry-the-one math, by hand, every single week.

I was probably ten or eleven when I looked at that and thought there had to be a better way to do it. So I opened Google Sheets and wrote my first formula.

After that I started getting up on Sundays too. I'd enter every order into a spreadsheet I had color-coded within an inch of its life, print it out, and hand it to her, so she could see at a glance what each person owed and exactly how many banana breads and scones the morning needed. Two of us awake before dawn instead of one.

That's still the best explanation I have for why I like this work. It was such a simple tool. But I could watch it make something easier for someone I love, the same morning I built it.

The tools have gotten more complicated since. Lately they look like a 10,000-record dataset of who does and doesn't get rooftop solar, a backend built from nothing for an early-stage platform, and 30 lab assistants supporting a course of 300 students. The motivation hasn't really moved. I want to build things that solve a real problem for real people, and I want to be close enough to see whether they actually did. What I'm after is still what my mom got that first Sunday: to look at the thing once and know exactly where she stood.

Applied AI and measurement

Getting a defensible number out of messy input, whether that input is rider complaints or a photograph of a card table, and being clear about what the number does not cover.

Project

2026

MIT 2.671

Card-Recognition Computer Vision Pipeline

OpenCV · ArUco · Python

A pipeline that recovers the full 52-card order of a deck from a single photograph and scores shuffle randomness by inversion count across four shuffle methods: riffle, overhand, machine auto, and machine manual.

  • Attached ArUco markers to all 52 cards, detected marker positions in an image, grouped detections into four horizontal rows, and compared the observed permutation against a canonical reference deck to quantify disorder.
  • Classical computer vision and measurement engineering rather than a trained model: fiducial detection plus geometric sorting and combinatorics, with no neural network or classifier involved.
  • Known limits, from my own README: detection assumes four horizontal rows, and the batch logic is hard-coded to the riffle dataset.
  • This is phase one of ReadyPlayerFour, a card-playing robotics project. The perception half is built and public. The manipulation half is scoped, with hardware not yet built.

Team project

Sentiment on the Line: MBTA

BERTopic · RoBERTa · Python

Transit agencies have enormous volumes of unstructured rider complaints and no systematic way to rank what actually matters.

  • Our team built a pipeline that scraped and deduplicated Reddit rider posts, modeled topics with BERTopic, and scored sentiment with RoBERTa.
  • I drafted the monitoring-design methodology, defined and tested the topic-severity formula, explored how engagement should complement sentiment rather than substitute for it, and wrote the extensions section.

  • GTFS, outage, and weather integration were scoped as future work and never built.
